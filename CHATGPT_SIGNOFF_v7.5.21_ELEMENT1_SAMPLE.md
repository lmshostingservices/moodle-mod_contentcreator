### TOPIC 1: Understanding Work Requirements (PC 1.1)

#### Card 1: KNOWLEDGE
**Title:** Reading Work Plans Before You Climb

**Description:** Before starting any work at heights, you must understand exactly what the job involves. Work plans tell you what needs to be done, where, and how to do it safely.

**Requirements:**
1. Locate the SWMS for your specific task before accessing the work area
2. Identify the exact work location, height, and duration from the work plan
3. Confirm the scope includes all tasks you'll perform at height
4. Check that the work plan matches current site conditions
5. Verify the plan is current and approved as per site procedures

**Do's:**
- Read the complete SWMS before starting work
- Ask your supervisor if any details are unclear
- Check the plan covers your specific trade tasks

**Don'ts:**
- Assume yesterday's plan still applies today
- Skip reading because you've done similar work before
- Start work if the plan doesn't match site conditions

**Document Reference:** SWMS-HAW-001 - "Step 1: Complete pre-start inspection of all equipment"

---

#### Card 2: SCENARIO
**Title:** The Missing Scope

**Situation:** You're a carpenter assigned to install roof battens on a new house. Your supervisor hands you a SWMS for 'general roofing work' but it doesn't mention batten installation. The other crew members are already on the roof.

**Complication:** The foreman says 'Just get up there, the SWMS covers roofing.' You notice the SWMS was created for tile laying, not carpentry work, and doesn't include the power tools you'll be using.

**Decision Point:** What should you do before climbing onto the roof?

**Mental Model:** Plan-Do-Check - The PLAN must match the DO - if your work isn't in the plan, the controls might not protect you.

---

#### Card 3: DECISION (Single Best Answer MCQ)
**Question:** Based on the scenario, what is the BEST course of action before starting work?

| Option | Answer | Feedback |
|--------|--------|----------|
| A | Proceed to the roof since the SWMS covers 'general roofing work' and you're experienced | INCORRECT - Experience doesn't replace proper documentation. The SWMS must specifically cover your tasks and equipment. |
| B | Request the supervisor update the SWMS to include batten installation and your power tools before you start | CORRECT - PC 1.1 requires work requirements match the work plan. An incomplete SWMS means incomplete risk controls. |
| C | Complete the work quickly then ask for the SWMS to be updated afterwards | INCORRECT - Working without proper controls exposes you to uncontrolled risks. Updates must happen before work begins. |
| D | Sign the SWMS anyway since the foreman approved it | INCORRECT - Your signature confirms the controls are adequate for YOUR work. You share legal responsibility. |

---

#### Card 4: FEEDBACK
**Correct Explanation:** Requesting an updated SWMS ensures all hazards from your specific work are identified and controlled. Under WHS law, you have the right - and responsibility - to ensure the SWMS covers your actual tasks.

**Incorrect Consequence:** Working under an inadequate SWMS means power tool hazards, fall distances, and rescue procedures for carpentry work haven't been assessed. If an incident occurs, investigators will find the SWMS didn't match the work performed.

**Key Takeaway:** Your SWMS must match your work scope exactly. Never sign off on a plan that doesn't cover what you're actually doing.

**Document Grounding:** SWMS-HAW-001 requires task-specific risk assessment before work commences

### TOPIC 2: Identifying Hazards and Selecting Controls (PC 1.2)

#### Card 1: KNOWLEDGE
**Title:** Finding Hazards Before They Find You

**Description:** Every height work task has hazards that can cause serious injury or death. Your job is to identify them with your team before anyone climbs up.

**Requirements:**
1. Walk the work area at ground level to identify overhead and underfoot hazards
2. Consult with your work team about hazards they've experienced on similar tasks
3. Check the JHA for known hazards specific to this work location
4. Consider changing conditions: weather, nearby work, public access
5. Discuss control options with your supervisor before finalising the approach

**Do's:**
- Complete a team toolbox talk identifying hazards for this specific job
- Use the JHA as a starting point, not the final answer
- Speak up if you notice something that wasn't in the risk assessment

**Don'ts:**
- Rely only on your own experience - the team sees more together
- Assume hazards are the same as yesterday
- Stay quiet if you're uncomfortable with the controls selected

**Document Reference:** JHA-ROOF-015 - "Hazards: Falls from height, fragile surfaces, weather conditions"

---

#### Card 2: SCENARIO
**Title:** The New Hazard

**Situation:** Your team is setting up to replace guttering on a two-storey building. You completed the JHA last week and everyone signed off. This morning, you notice the building next door has started demolition work - there's dust, noise, and occasional debris falling near your work area.

**Complication:** The supervisor says the JHA was done properly and there's pressure to start because the client is waiting. One of your crew suggests 'just keeping an eye on the demo work' as a control.

**Decision Point:** The demolition work wasn't in your original JHA. What should happen next?

**Mental Model:** Dynamic Risk Assessment - Site conditions change daily. Your risk controls must change with them.

---

#### Card 3: DECISION (Single Best Answer MCQ)
**Question:** What is the BEST response to the changed site conditions?

| Option | Answer | Feedback |
|--------|--------|----------|
| A | Proceed with extra caution since the JHA was properly completed last week | INCORRECT - A JHA captures hazards at a point in time. New hazards require new controls. |
| B | Stop work, update the JHA to include demolition hazards, and agree new controls with the team and supervisor | CORRECT - PC 1.2 requires hazard identification in consultation with the team. New hazards = new consultation. |
| C | Move to the opposite side of the building away from the demolition | INCORRECT - This might help, but without documenting the hazard and controls, you're working outside your risk assessment. |
| D | Ask the demolition crew to stop while you complete your work | INCORRECT - You can't control another contractor's work. Your controls must manage the hazard on your side. |

---

#### Card 4: FEEDBACK
**Correct Explanation:** Stopping to update the JHA isn't being difficult - it's being professional. Demolition creates airborne debris, noise distraction, and potential structural vibration. All of these affect your height work. The team consultation ensures everyone knows the new risks.

**Incorrect Consequence:** Working with unassessed hazards means your existing controls (harnesses, edge protection) might not be enough. A piece of demolition debris striking a worker on a ladder isn't covered by fall prevention equipment.

**Key Takeaway:** When conditions change, your risk assessment must change. Stop, consult, update, then work.

**Document Grounding:** JHA-ROOF-015 explicitly requires consideration of 'nearby work' as a hazard category

---

#### Card 5: QUICK-CHECK
**Checklist:**

- [ ] We have consulted with the supervisor on control measures (PC 1.2)
- [ ] Controls are selected based on the hierarchy of control (PC 1.2)
- [ ] Changed conditions have been reassessed (PC 1.2)

**Terminology:**
- **Hierarchy of Control:** The ranked order of control effectiveness: Elimination, Substitution, Isolation, Engineering, Administration, PPE. Always start at the top.

### TOPIC 3: Equipment Selection and Checking (PC 1.3)

#### Card 1: KNOWLEDGE
**Title:** Checking Equipment That Keeps You Alive

**Description:** Height safety equipment is the last line of defence between you and a fall. Every piece must be inspected before use - your life depends on it.

**Requirements:**
1. Select equipment rated for the task: load capacity, fall distance, anchor strength
2. Complete a visual inspection of harnesses, lanyards, and connectors before each use
3. Check for current inspection tags on scaffolds and elevated work platforms
4. Verify all equipment meets Australian Standards as marked on labels
5. Report and remove defective equipment from service immediately

**Do's:**
- Use the SOP-EQ-042 checklist for systematic equipment inspection
- Check webbing for cuts, abrasion, chemical damage, or UV degradation
- Confirm all carabiners and hooks lock properly and gates spring closed

**Don'ts:**
- Use equipment without an inspection tag or with an expired tag
- Assume equipment is fine because it was used yesterday
- Put defective equipment back in storage 'for someone else to check'

**Document Reference:** SOP-EQ-042 - "Daily inspection required before use. Check: base plates level, bracing secure, platforms complete, guardrails at 900-1100mm, toe boards in place"

---

#### Card 2: SCENARIO
**Title:** The Frayed Lanyard

**Situation:** You're about to start work on a scaffold at 8 metres. During your pre-start harness check, you notice the webbing on your lanyard has some minor fraying near the stitching. The damage is small - about 10mm of surface wear.

**Complication:** The only spare lanyard on site is a different type that connects differently to your harness. Your supervisor is in a meeting and the job has a deadline. Your workmate says 'That tiny bit of wear won't matter for one day.'

**Decision Point:** The frayed lanyard is the equipment you've been issued. What should you do?

**Mental Model:** Critical Safety Equipment Standard - Height safety equipment has zero tolerance for damage - any defect could mean complete failure under load.

---

#### Card 3: DECISION (Single Best Answer MCQ)
**Question:** What is the correct response to the damaged lanyard?

| Option | Answer | Feedback |
|--------|--------|----------|
| A | Use the frayed lanyard carefully since the damage is minor and only for today | INCORRECT - Any visible damage to fall arrest equipment requires immediate removal from service. 'Minor' damage can cause complete failure. |
| B | Tag the lanyard as defective, wait for supervisor guidance on the alternate equipment or replacement | CORRECT - PC 1.3 requires equipment be checked for serviceability. Defective equipment must be removed and reported before alternative arrangements are made. |
| C | Use the different lanyard type even though the connection is unfamiliar | INCORRECT - Using unfamiliar equipment without training creates its own hazard. Proper equipment matching and competency is required. |
| D | Work without the lanyard since the scaffold has guardrails | INCORRECT - Guardrails and harnesses serve different purposes. Your SWMS likely requires both for this height. |

---

#### Card 4: FEEDBACK
**Correct Explanation:** Tagging and removing the lanyard protects you and every future user. Waiting for supervisor guidance ensures you get compatible equipment and proper instruction. The delay is far less costly than a fall.

**Incorrect Consequence:** Fall arrest forces can be extreme — damaged fibres may fail instantly under load. The 'minor' wear you dismissed could be the difference between an arrested fall and a fatality.

**Key Takeaway:** If equipment shows ANY damage, tag it out. Never use compromised height safety gear - there's no 'safe enough' level of damage.

**Document Grounding:** SOP-EQ-042 requires removal of damaged equipment from service and immediate reporting

---

- [ ] I have completed visual inspection before use (PC 1.3)
- [ ] All tags and Australian Standards markings are present and current (PC 1.3)

**Terminology:**
- **Serviceability:** Equipment is in proper working condition with no damage, wear, or defects that could affect its performance. Verified through systematic inspection.

### TOPIC 4: Confirming Access and Egress Routes (PC 1.4)

#### Card 1: KNOWLEDGE
**Title:** Planning Your Way Up and Down

**Description:** How you get to your work position matters as much as the work itself. Safe access and emergency egress routes must be confirmed before you start.

**Requirements:**
1. Identify the primary access route to the work area and confirm it's clear
2. Locate the emergency egress route and verify it remains accessible
3. Confirm ladder angles meet the 4:1 ratio (1 out for every 4 up)
4. Ensure scaffold access stairs or ladders have handrails and are unobstructed
5. Verify the rescue plan access points align with the routes you'll use

**Do's:**
- Walk the access route before climbing to check for obstructions
- Keep three points of contact when using ladders or access stairs
- Maintain access routes clear of tools, materials, and debris during work

**Don'ts:**
- Climb external scaffold frames instead of using internal access ladders
- Block the egress route with materials or equipment
- Assume someone else has checked the access route

**Document Reference:** SWMS-HAW-001 - "Step 3: Confirm rescue plan with supervisor before ascending"

---

#### Card 2: SCENARIO
**Title:** The Blocked Ladder

**Situation:** You're ready to access a scaffold for afternoon work. The internal access ladder on Level 2 has been blocked by another trade storing plasterboard sheets across the landing. They've gone to lunch and won't be back for 30 minutes.

**Complication:** Your work is time-critical and your supervisor said to get started. The external scaffold bracing looks climbable, and you've seen others use it as a shortcut. The proper access is blocked but you could work around it.

**Decision Point:** The formal access route is obstructed. What is the safe approach?

**Mental Model:** Access Equals Emergency Exit - Your access route is also your emergency egress. If you can't get down safely, you can't get up safely.

---

#### Card 3: DECISION (Single Best Answer MCQ)
**Question:** What should you do when the formal access route is blocked?

| Option | Answer | Feedback |
|--------|--------|----------|
| A | Climb the external scaffold bracing since others do it and it's only temporary | INCORRECT - External bracing is not designed for climbing. It lacks handholds, is not load-rated for climbing, and often has pinch points. |
| B | Wait until the obstruction is cleared or find an alternative formal access route before ascending | CORRECT - PC 1.4 requires confirmed access routes. Obstructed routes must be cleared or alternatives identified before work begins. |
| C | Move the plasterboard yourself to clear the access | INCORRECT - Moving another trade's materials creates liability issues and potential manual handling hazards if done incorrectly. |
| D | Start work on Level 1 instead and do Level 2 later | INCORRECT - This changes your work plan without consultation. Your SWMS may specify a work sequence for safety reasons. |

---

#### Card 4: FEEDBACK
**Correct Explanation:** Waiting or finding an alternative formal route ensures your access doubles as emergency egress. If an incident occurred and the route was blocked, rescue would be delayed. The 30-minute wait is a small investment in your safety.

**Incorrect Consequence:** Workers who climb scaffold bracing risk falls from unexpected failure, hand injuries from pinch points, and disciplinary action for unsafe work practices. More critically, if you're injured at height with the access blocked, rescuers face the same obstruction.

**Key Takeaway:** Never improvise access. If your planned route is blocked, solve the problem before you climb - your egress depends on it.

**Document Grounding:** SWMS-HAW-001 requires confirmed rescue plan access points before ascending

---

- [ ] Rescue plan access points align with my routes (PC 1.4)

**Terminology:**
- **Egress:** The route used to exit a work area, especially during emergencies. Must remain clear and accessible at all times during work at heights.

**Reflection:** Could you evacuate your current work area within 2 minutes if needed? What would slow you down?
